How it works, in plain words

The pool runs two systems, each a set of fixed rules run by a computer. They watch liquid markets around the clock and only act when their conditions are met. No guessing and no emotions. Half of the money is with each system.

1
You join the pool

Your ₱1,000 buys a slice of one shared trading pool. When the pool grows or shrinks, your slice moves by the same percent as everyone else's.

2
The loss is limited before every trade

Every trade has an exit point for a loss, set before the trade is opened. Each trade puts 2.5% of its system's money at risk.

3
The 20% fee on new profit only

Every two months, 20% of new profit above your best level so far is the fee. A loss means no fee, and a fee is never charged twice on the same profit.

What to expect

  • Most trades are small losses. Only about 35% of trades win. Money is made because the wins are much bigger than the losses.
  • Some months are red, sometimes several in a row. That is normal for this kind of system. Page 9 shows the bad stretches.
  • The swings are big. With 2.5% put at risk per trade, an investor's balance once fell −51% from its high before recovering, and the worst single month lost 29%.
  • It does best when markets move strongly (big rallies or big sell-offs) and slowly gives back a little when prices drift sideways.
  • All trading costs are included: exchange fees, slippage (getting a slightly worse price than planned), funding charges and other costs. The 20% fee is on top and already taken out of every peso figure here.
How the compounding works. We start with ₱1,000 on 1 January 2020 (the first trade came in March 2020). Each system keeps its own balance, and every trade puts 2.5% of that balance at risk, measured when the trade opens. Wins make the next trade a little bigger and losses make it smaller, so profit is re-invested trade by trade. The 20% fee is taken every two months (end of February, April, June, August, October and December) from profit above your best level so far. The last, partial month is settled on the last day of data.

How these numbers were made, and what they leave out

Method
  • The trades. The real trading rules of the two systems, unchanged, replayed on stored 4-hour price candles for five liquid markets from 2020 to 20 October 2026. Exchange fees, slippage and funding charges are already inside every trade's result.
  • The money. The ₱1,000 is split in half between the two systems. Each trade puts 2.5% of its own system's balance at risk when it opens, and the profit or loss is booked when the trade closes. Trades from all markets share the system's balance, and are processed in time order.
  • The fee. 20% of profit above the investor's best level so far, taken at the end of every even month, and on the last day of data for the last partial month. A loss means no fee, and the best level only moves up.
  • Calendar. A month counts the trades that closed in it. Year tables use a calendar year and carry the balance over. Trades still open at the end of the data are not counted.
What it leaves out
  • Hindsight. The markets and the rule settings were chosen on this same history. Treat the figures as an upper bound, not a forecast.
  • No ceiling on trade size. The test lets each trade grow with the balance. In real trading a cap on how much is risked per trade, and the room that the markets have to take larger orders, would slow growth once the balance is large. The big late-year pesos here would not be reached in the same way.
  • Small accounts. Exchanges sell whole contracts. A ₱1,000 slice of the pool is not traded on its own; it buys part of one pool. The pool's early trades would have been lumpier than shown.
  • Trades open together. Several trades can be open at once and are sized from the same balance, so a real run can fall a little further than shown.
  • Late or skipped trades. Trades the live system skips because margin is short, or enters late, are not modelled.
  • Two systems, one story. A few giant winning trades carry a large part of the result. Take them out and the totals look much smaller.

Closing remarks

Researched and backtested by James Rafael Boroy (Est. Oct 20, 2026), by replaying the trading rules on Binance and MEXC futures market data from March 2020 to 20 October 2026.

These results are pure statistics: they show how the system behaved on past prices, and how likely it is to behave that way again. They are not a forecast. Markets are unpredictable and change over time, so the future can look very different from this test, and the figures here may change as new data comes in. Nothing in this report is guaranteed.
